Elliptic curve certificates

A Pocklington certificate needs the factorisation of n−1n - 1n−1, and for most large primes that is out of reach. An elliptic curve gives a second chance, and a third, and as many as needed: any curve modulo nnn whose number of points has a large prime factor will do. This is how primes of hundreds of digits are proven in practice.

The theorem

Let nnn be prime to 6, EEE a curve y2=x3+ax+by^2 = x^3 + ax + by2=x3+ax+b modulo nnn, and PPP a point on it. Suppose m=kqm = kqm=kq with qqq prime and

q>(n1/4+1)2,m⋅P=O,k⋅P≠O (a finite point, with every division along the way possible). q > \left(n^{1/4} + 1\right)^2, \qquad m\cdot P = O, \qquad k\cdot P \ne O \ \text{(a finite point, with every division along the way possible)}. q>(n1/4+1)2,m⋅P=O,k⋅P=O (a finite point, with every division along the way possible).

Then nnn is prime (Goldwasser and Kilian, 1986). The reason is short. If nnn had a prime factor p≤np \le \sqrt np≤n​, then modulo ppp the point kPkPkP would have order exactly qqq in E(Fp)E(\mathbb{F}_p)E(Fp​). But that group has at most (p+1)2≤(n1/4+1)2<q(\sqrt p + 1)^2 \le (n^{1/4} + 1)^2 < q(p​+1)2≤(n1/4+1)2<q elements, by Hasse's theorem. So the certificate is the curve, the point, mmm and qqq, together with a certificate for qqq, which is about the size of n/kn/kn/k: a chain that descends until Pocklington or trial division finishes it.

Where the curve comes from

Goldwasser and Kilian picked random curves and counted their points with Schoof's algorithm, which is far too slow to do many times. Atkin and Morain turned it around: build a curve whose number of points is known in advance.

What has to be trusted

Very little. The class polynomial is computed numerically, square roots modulo nnn assume nnn is prime, and the search takes shortcuts. None of that matters for soundness: all of it only serves to find a curve and a point, and the certificate is then checked by the independent verifier, which knows nothing about complex multiplication. It checks that the point is on the curve, that the discriminant of the curve is a unit modulo nnn, the size of qqq, the certificate of qqq, and the two multiplications. Its curve arithmetic uses plain affine coordinates and insists that every divisor is a unit modulo nnn, so each step is a true group operation modulo every prime factor of nnn at once; a shortcut through projective coordinates would not have that property. A mistake in the search can only mean that no certificate is found.

The tests attack the verifier directly: they build composite numbers n=p rn = p\,rn=pr, curves and points whose orders modulo both primes are known, and offer every prime factor of the order as qqq. All are rejected.

How far it goes

Discriminants up to 5000 with class number up to 12 are tried by default (options maxDiscriminant, maxClassNumber). Primes of 60 digits take a fraction of a second, 150 digits a couple of seconds, 230 digits under ten seconds. The search can fail, and then provePrime says so and isPrime refuses, as before.
