Factoring

Splitting a number into primes: trial division for the small factors, Pollard's rho for the medium ones, and the p − 1 method when a factor happens to have a smooth neighbour. Every prime in the answer says whether it is proven.

The whole factorisation

import { factor, formatFactors } from "@ratmath/numtheory";

formatFactors(factor(600851475143n));   // "71 · 839 · 1471 · 6857"
formatFactors(factor(2n ** 64n + 1n));  // "274177 · 67280421310721": Landry's 1880 split of F₆
formatFactors(factor(10n ** 20n + 1n)); // "73 · 137 · 1676321 · 5964848081"
factor(2n ** 67n - 1n);
// [{ prime: 193707721n, exponent: 1, proven: true },
//  { prime: 761838257287n, exponent: 1, proven: true }]

factor divides out the primes below \(10^5\), then splits what is left: a probable prime goes into the answer, a perfect square is split into its roots, anything else goes to Pollard's rho, then to \(p - 1\), then to the elliptic curve method. Factors below \(2^{64}\) are proven by Baillie–PSW; a larger one is proven by a certificate, and proven: false says when that could not be found.

Pollard's rho

Iterate \(x \mapsto x^2 + c \pmod n\). Modulo an unknown prime \(p \mid n\) the sequence lives in a set of size \(p\), so it must repeat, and by the birthday paradox it does so after about \(\sqrt{p}\) steps: its picture is the Greek letter \(\rho\), a tail and a loop. Once \(x_i \equiv x_j \pmod p\), \(\gcd(x_i - x_j, n)\) is a multiple of \(p\). Brent's version finds the loop by comparing against a saved value at powers of two, and multiplies many differences together before taking one gcd. The cost is about \(\sqrt p\) multiplications: a 12-digit factor takes around a million.

pollardRho(1000000007n * 998244353n);   // 998244353n
pollardPm1(1000000007n * 1000000009n);  // 1000000009n
pollardPm1(2n ** 67n - 1n);             // null: see below

Pollard's p − 1

If \(p - 1\) has only prime factors below a bound \(B\), then \(p - 1\) divides \(M = \prod_{q \le B} q^{\lfloor \log_q B \rfloor}\), and by Fermat \(a^M \equiv 1 \pmod p\), so \(\gcd(a^M - 1, n)\) picks \(p\) out. It finds 1000000009 in \(1000000007 \cdot 1000000009\) at once, because \(1000000008 = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 7 \cdot 109^2 \cdot 167\) is smooth while \(1000000006 = 2 \cdot 500000003\) is not. It can also be too lucky: for Cole's number \(2^{67} - 1\) both \(p - 1\)s are smooth (\(193707720 = 2^3 \cdot 3^3 \cdot 5 \cdot 67 \cdot 2677\)), both factors drop out together, the gcd is \(n\) itself, and the method reports nothing.

Beyond rho

Rho slows down when the smallest prime factor passes about 12 digits. The seventh Fermat number \(2^{128} + 1\) is the product of two primes of 17 and 22 digits: rho would need hundreds of millions of steps, and \(p - 1\) fails on it. So after a short run of rho and \(p - 1\), factor goes on to the elliptic curve method, which is to \(p - 1\) what a family of groups is to one group, and which finds that 17-digit factor in a fraction of a second. Factors of up to about 25 digits are within reach; beyond that factor gives up with the reason rather than running for hours.
